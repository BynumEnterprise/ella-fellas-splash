Twelve tracks into Dandelion, Ella Langley does something no other song on the album does: she steps aside and sings someone else's words. Her cover of "It Wasn't God Who Made Honky Tonk Angels" is the only true cover on the record, and if you've wondered why Ella Langley reached back to a 1952 Kitty Wells song to anchor the biggest album of her career, the short answer is that she's pointing straight at the woman who made that career possible. This is the song that handed country music its first female No. 1 — and Ella knows exactly what she's doing by singing it.

## The Quick Answer: Why Ella Langley Covered "Honky Tonk Angels"

Langley didn't pick a deep cut to show off. She picked a landmark. "It Wasn't God Who Made Honky Tonk Angels" is the record that broke open the door every woman in country music has walked through since, and putting it on Dandelion is Ella's way of saying out loud whose shoulders she's standing on. She's been folding it into her live set on the road, too, so the studio version isn't a one-off novelty — it's a tribute she performs on purpose, night after night, as [Whiskey Riff laid out when it traced the history behind the cover](https://www.whiskeyriff.com/2026/04/02/ella-langley-honors-generations-of-country-icons-with-it-wasnt-god-who-made-honky-tonk-angels-cover-the-history-behind-the-song/).

There's a neat rhyme in it, too. Ella spent 2025 and 2026 setting her own "first woman to…" records — most of them tied to ["Choosin' Texas" and its historic Hot 100 run](/news/2026-07-22-choosin-texas-meaning). Covering the song that produced the very first female No. 1 in country history isn't an accident of tracklisting. It's a woman who keeps breaking records tipping her hat to the woman who set the first one.

## The 1952 Original: Country's First Female No. 1

Here's the history, because the cover doesn't land without it. Kitty Wells recorded "It Wasn't God Who Made Honky Tonk Angels" in May 1952, written by J.D. "Jay" Miller. It was an answer song — a direct reply to Hank Thompson's "The Wild Side of Life," in which a man mourns that his bride left him for the barroom and shrugs that "I didn't know God made honky tonk angels." Wells' record flips the blame back: for every woman who went wrong, there was usually a married man who told her he was single. It blamed unfaithful men for creating unfaithful women, and in the conservative, male-run country of 1952, that was a daring thing to put on a record.

It worked. The song spent six weeks at No. 1 and became the first Billboard country chart-topper by a solo woman, launching the little-known Wells into stardom and, as the [song's full history records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It_Wasn%27t_God_Who_Made_Honky_Tonk_Angels), clearing a path for Patsy Cline, Loretta Lynn, Tammy Wynette and Dolly Parton. It's since gone into the Grammy Hall of Fame and the National Recording Registry, and Rolling Stone ranked it No. 11 on its 200 Greatest Country Songs of All Time. The part fans love most: Wells almost didn't cut it. She was ready to step away from music and only agreed to the session for the standard $125 union pay.

## Why It Was Banned

The record's success came with a fight. NBC's radio network banned the song as "suggestive," and Wells was barred from performing it on the Grand Ole Opry and on NBC's "Prince Albert" program; the BBC blacklisted it across the Atlantic. A song we now file under "classic" was, at the time, too pointed for the gatekeepers — a woman naming men as the problem was more than the format wanted to broadcast. That's the charge Ella is plugging back into when she sings it: not a dusty standard, but a song that once got a woman thrown off the biggest stage in country music for telling the truth.

## What Ella Langley's Version Adds

Langley plays it mostly straight, and that restraint is the point. She keeps the melody and the message intact rather than modernizing them, which lets a 2026 arena crowd hear a 1952 lyric land exactly as written. [Holler's breakdown of Ella's version](https://holler.country/lyrics/it-wasnt-god-who-made-honky-tonk-angels-by-ella-langley-lyrics-and-meaning/) frames it as a torch-passing moment — a nod to every woman who paved the way and handed the baton to the generation now filling the charts. Her cover charted, too, reaching No. 83 on the Hot 100 and cracking the country top 40 at No. 32, which is a strange and wonderful thing to say about a seven-decade-old song.

What it adds to Dandelion is context. Drop a 1952 protest song into the middle of an album full of Ella's own writing and the whole record reads differently: her confidence, her records, her right to be blunt about men in a lyric all trace back through a line of women, and she's making that line audible.

## Where It Sits on Dandelion

Dandelion is a record about a year of enormous change, and Track 12 is where Ella zooms out from her own story to the century that produced it. Put the Kitty Wells cover next to the family-and-grit of ["Bottom of Your Boots"](/news/2026-08-11-ella-langley-bottom-of-your-boots-meaning) and the record-smashing ["Choosin' Texas,"](/news/2026-07-22-choosin-texas-meaning) and you can see what she's arguing across [the full Dandelion album](/songs/dandelion): that the new wave of country women didn't come from nowhere. If you're catching a stop on the [Dandelion Tour](/tour), watch for this one in the set — it's the moment the show quietly becomes a history lesson.
