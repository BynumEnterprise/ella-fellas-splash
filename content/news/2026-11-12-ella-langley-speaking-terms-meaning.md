If you are here trying to work out what Ella Langley's "Speaking Terms" means, the short version is that it is a faith song about fear — the fear that she and God had quietly stopped talking, that they were no longer on speaking terms. It is one of the most vulnerable moments on her 2026 album *Dandelion*, and it almost was not hers at all. On the final night of the Dandelion Tour in St. Paul, Langley told the crowd that Beyoncé and Cody Johnson were among the artists who had the song on hold before she fought to record it herself, [as Whiskey Riff reported](https://www.whiskeyriff.com/2026/11/09/ella-langley-reveals-speaking-terms-was-on-hold-by-beyonce-cody-johnson-more-before-she-recorded-it-for-dandelion/).

## The Quick Answer: What "Speaking Terms" Means

"Speaking Terms" is about drifting away from faith and being afraid it has gone quiet for good. The title plays on the everyday phrase: when two people fall out, we say they are "not on speaking terms," and Langley turns that on her relationship with God. The song sits in the worry that the line has gone dead — that she has let too much distance build to feel heard anymore. Langley has been open that the writing came out of a real season of doubt and prayer, and [Whiskey Riff described the track as her getting "extremely vulnerable" about her relationship with God](https://www.whiskeyriff.com/2026/04/15/ella-langley-gets-extremely-vulnerable-about-her-relationship-with-god-on-beautiful-new-song-speaking-terms/). On *Dandelion* it reads as the low point before the turn, the honest admission that has to come before anything can grow back.

## Who Wrote "Speaking Terms" — and the Artists Who Passed On It

"Speaking Terms" was written by Joybeth Taylor and Helene Cronin, not by Langley herself — and that is where the story gets interesting. Taylor is a close friend and frequent collaborator who also had a hand in Langley's first Hot 100 No. 1, ["Choosin' Texas"](/songs/choosin-texas), and Cronin has written about the song finding its way onto the record on [her own songwriting blog](https://helenecronin.com/home/blog/landing-a-song-on-ella-langley-s-new-record-dandelion).

Because Taylor and Cronin pitched it widely, the demo made the rounds at the top of the industry before Langley got to it. Telling the St. Paul crowd the backstory, Langley said plainly, "Multiple artists had this one on hold," and then named names: "Beyoncé had this song on hold for a second, believe it or not," and "Cody Johnson — and there's a lot of people that this song didn't land on," [per Whiskey Riff's account of the night](https://www.whiskeyriff.com/2026/11/09/ella-langley-reveals-speaking-terms-was-on-hold-by-beyonce-cody-johnson-more-before-she-recorded-it-for-dandelion/). Exactly when Beyoncé's camp held the song is not something Langley spelled out, and the suggestion that it was tied to a specific project is a reader's guess rather than anything she confirmed.

## "I Just Wanted The Song To Win": How Langley Landed It

What makes the story land is that Langley did not grab the song the first time it crossed her desk — she waited until she felt she had earned it. "I just wanted the song to win, and I didn't feel like at the time I was worth enough for it until I had that moment," she told the crowd, [as Holler recounted](https://holler.country/news/breaking/ella-langley-says-beyonc-and-cody-johnson-nearly-recorded-speaking-terms-first-i-just-wanted-the-song-to-win/). Once she was ready, she called Taylor directly and asked, in her telling, "Hey, can I put that song on hold?" — and her friend said yes.

It is a revealing way to think about a cut that so many bigger names circled: Langley did not want to be the artist who simply out-bid the room for a hit. She wanted the version of herself who could do the song justice, and she held off until she believed she was that person. For a track about repairing a relationship with God, that patience is the whole point.

## Where "Speaking Terms" Sits On Dandelion

On the album, "Speaking Terms" is the confession that sets up the recovery. It leads into ["Butterfly Season"](/songs/butterfly-season), the Miranda Lambert duet about shedding an old version of yourself, so the two songs work as a pair — the fear first, then the growth. It also rhymes thematically with ["Be Her"](/songs/be-her), Langley's self-worth ballad about the grounded, faith-steady woman she is trying to become, and it is the quiet flip side of the record's louder, angrier moments like ["Hell at Night"](/songs/hell-at-night). Taken together, they are why *Dandelion* plays less like a singles collection and more like one person working something out in real time — an album that [debuted at No. 1 on the Billboard 200, per Whiskey Riff](https://www.whiskeyriff.com/2026/11/09/ella-langley-reveals-speaking-terms-was-on-hold-by-beyonce-cody-johnson-more-before-she-recorded-it-for-dandelion/) and has since gone multi-platinum.
