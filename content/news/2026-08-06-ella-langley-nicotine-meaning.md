If you have ever tried to walk away from a person the way you would try to quit a habit, Ella Langley's "Nicotine" already knows how that goes. The song takes the language of addiction and points it straight at a relationship, and the meaning of "Nicotine" lives in that one uncomfortable comparison: this is a love the narrator knows is bad for her and reaches for anyway. It is one of the quieter cuts fans latched onto from her 2024 record *hungover*, and it is still one of the most-requested songs when her name comes up.

## What "Nicotine" Is Really About

The central image does all the work. Instead of asking a lover to stay, Langley asks them to *be* the thing she is hooked on: "put you to my lips and get a fix, baby, be my nicotine." That flips a cigarette, the classic symbol of a habit you cannot shake, into a stand-in for a person. The comfort, the craving, the knowledge that it is not good for you and the total inability to stop, all of it maps onto both a smoke and a relationship, which is exactly why the metaphor lands.

[Songs Magazine reads the track](https://www.songsmagazine.com/country/2024/05/09/nicotine-by-ella-langley-a-musical-journey-into-love-and-farewell/) as a story of love and dependency that burns like cigarette smoke and refuses to be resisted. That is the tension Langley keeps taut for three and a half minutes: she is not celebrating the addiction, but she is not pretending she can quit it either. It is a love song and a warning at the same time.

## Where "Nicotine" Fits on 'Hungover'

"Nicotine" arrived as an early taste of *hungover*, Langley's [2024 debut studio album](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungover_(album)) that later expanded into the *Still Hungover* edition. That record is where most people first met her as a songwriter, and it holds two of her signatures: the Riley Green duet ["You Look Like You Love Me,"](/songs/you-look-like-you-love-me) which became her breakout, and ["Weren't for the Wind,"](/songs/werent-for-the-wind) the ballad that showed how far her voice could stretch. "Nicotine" sits between those two modes: sharper than the ballads, more intimate than the barn-burners.

Heard next to the rest of the catalog, "Nicotine" reads like an early blueprint for what she does best. The same honesty about wanting the wrong thing runs through later cuts like ["Choosin' Texas,"](/songs/choosin-texas) which means the metaphor never felt like a gimmick, just Langley telling the truth in the only way that fit.
